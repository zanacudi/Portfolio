Diet

Eat well. Nourish your brain. Support your gut.

The idea running through the documentary is simple: the brain is an organ, and like every other organ it is built and maintained from what you eat. Food supplies more than energy — fats, fibre, vitamins, minerals and plant compounds all feed into how the brain works, and much of that traffic passes through the gut.

There is no single magic brain food. What appears to matter is the overall pattern: varied, mostly minimally processed, rich in plants and healthy fats. This guide turns that pattern into seven ordinary days of cooking.

Inspired by the documentary A Healthy Diet for a Healthy Brain, also released as Better Brain Health: We Are What We Eat, and by wider nutrition research.

What this plan claims — and what it doesn't

Diet is associated with brain and cognitive health across large population studies, and some of the mechanisms (omega-3 fats in cell membranes, fibre feeding gut bacteria, B vitamins in nerve function) are well established. Much of the rest — particularly the gut–brain axis and the microbiome's role in mood — is active, promising, still-developing research rather than settled fact.

So: no food here will make you smarter, prevent dementia, or treat depression, and none is presented that way. What a pattern like this reliably does is supply the nutrients a brain needs, in a way that is also good for the heart and the rest of the body. If you have a diagnosed condition, are pregnant, or take medication, talk to a clinician or dietitian before changing how you eat.

Six principles for brain-friendly eating

Everything in the week ahead comes back to these. Get the pattern right and the individual meals become interchangeable.

Eat a variety of whole foods

The brain draws on dozens of nutrients, and no single food carries them all. Variety across the week does more than perfecting any one meal.

Fill the plate with colour

Different pigments in vegetables and fruit come with different plant compounds. Aim for several colours a day rather than the same two vegetables on repeat.

Choose fats that work for you

Roughly 60% of the brain's dry weight is fat. Oily fish, olive oil, nuts and seeds supply the kinds of fat the body can't make well on its own.

Eat fibre at every meal

Legumes, whole grains, vegetables and fruit feed the bacteria in your gut. Most people eat around half the fibre they need.

Look after the gut

The gut and brain are in constant two-way conversation. Fermented foods like plain yogurt and kefir add to the diversity of what lives down there.

Lean away from ultra-processed

Not banned — just less often. Diets dominated by ultra-processed food tend to crowd out the fibre, fats and micronutrients everything above depends on.

Your seven days

Pick a day, open any meal for the method, and tick things off as you cook them. Swap days around freely — the pattern matters more than the order. Snacks aren't listed; fruit, plain yogurt, a handful of nuts or leftover hummus with vegetables all fit.

Why these foods keep appearing

Six groups do most of the work in the week above. None of them is a cure for anything; together they cover the nutrients the brain and body draw on.

Oily fish

Salmon, sardines, trout, mackerel, canned tuna

The main food source of EPA and DHA, the long-chain omega-3 fats concentrated in brain tissue. Two servings a week is the usual public-health advice.

Berries

Blueberries, strawberries, blackberries, raspberries

Fibre, vitamin C and the pigments called anthocyanins. Frozen berries are just as good nutritionally and cost far less out of season.

Leafy greens

Spinach, kale, arugula, chard, romaine

Dense in folate, vitamin K, lutein and magnesium for very few calories. The vegetable group most consistently linked with slower cognitive decline in observational studies.

Nuts and seeds

Walnuts, almonds, chia, flax, pumpkin seeds

Unsaturated fats, vitamin E, magnesium and fibre in a form that needs no preparation. A small handful is a portion.

Legumes

Lentils, chickpeas, black, kidney and white beans

The cheapest fibre and plant protein on the shelf, plus folate and iron. Canned and rinsed works fine when time is short.

Fermented foods

Plain yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso

They add live microbes and variety to the diet. The gut–brain research here is genuinely interesting and genuinely unfinished — eat them because they're good food, not as medicine.

More of this, less of that

Nothing on the right is banned. Birthday cake is still birthday cake. The question is what fills most plates, most weeks.

Room for plenty

Before you start

Change one meal at a time rather than the whole week at once — a swapped breakfast that lasts a year beats a perfect plan abandoned by Thursday. Costs stay manageable if you lean on lentils, eggs, frozen vegetables, frozen berries and canned fish, and save fresh salmon for once a week. No supplements are needed to follow this plan.
