1. From a lab number to a field reading

Module 1 built ψ from void ratio and mean effective stress — both things you control in a triaxial cell. In the ground, you get neither directly. You get a cone pushed at 2 cm/s, reporting tip resistance, sleeve friction, and pore pressure. This module solves the inverse problem: given what the cone measured, what ψ produced it? — and then asks the harder follow-up question every real site raises: which ψ do you actually design with, once you admit it varies?

Recommended route: mental model first, then dimensionless groups, soil type, the ψ inversion itself, and only then the variability question.
The big idea
Qp = k·exp(−mψ)

Cone resistance, once you strip out the effect of stress level, is an exponential function of state alone. Invert it, and a number in MPa becomes a statement about density relative to critical.

About this module

Module 1 built the mathematical core — the state parameter ψ and the NorSand model — from laboratory triaxial data. But you can't sample sand undisturbed; almost everything in practice comes from a cone penetrometer (CPT) pushed into the ground. Chapter 4 solves the resulting inverse problem: given a measured tip resistance, what ψ produced it? Chapter 5 then asks the uncomfortable follow-up question every real site raises — ψ is never one number, it's a distribution, so what single value should actually go into a design calculation?

This module continues directly from Module 1 (Chapters 1–3) — it reuses the same Table 2.1 sand database and the same ψ sign convention throughout. See also the companion Guide to Cone Penetration Testing module for Robertson's own direct CPT correlations, and Module 3 for what happens once a characteristic ψ is used to assess liquefaction risk.

Reading the log: depth increases downward, the way a real CPT log is normally plotted. The two shaded bands split the profile at about 30 m, where qt steps up into a firmer zone. About this sounding. The default profile above is a derived teaching dataset, not anyone's project data: it was resampled from ~2200 points onto a clean 0.25 m grid, rescaled, given a deterministic per-point perturbation, and had a few localized u2 spikes clipped, so it keeps a realistic shape and scatter while carrying none of the original values. Treat it as illustrative only. The ψ panel and Sections 5/7's calculators use a simple assumed unit weight and hydrostatic water table (adjustable above) to estimate σ′vo and u0 from depth alone; a real design would use an actual measured or estimated unit-weight profile instead. The −0.05 dilative/contractive line on the ψ panel is a practical rule of thumb used only for this log's shading and badge — the theoretical critical state is still exactly ψ=0, shown as the thin dashed line beside it. Pore pressure (u2, and the hydrostatic reference u0) is expressed as head of water, in m with ft in parentheses, matching how a real cone's piezometric channel is usually reported — not as kPa or psi. Other secondary units in parentheses (tsf, ft) use the standard cone conversion 1 tsf = 95.76 kPa; all internal calculations stay in metric (kPa) regardless of what's displayed.
Why does a deeper water table make the same reading look more contractive? Try it: drag the water-table slider deeper while watching the ψ panel at a fixed depth. A deeper water table means less pore pressure counteracts the overburden, so the estimated effective stress p′o at that depth goes up. Since the raw qt reading hasn't changed, Qp=(qt−p′o)/p′o goes down — the same absolute resistance now looks weaker relative to a higher confining stress — and the Section 7 inversion reads that as a more positive (more contractive) ψ. This is the exact same effect as Module 1's ψ=e−ec(p′): raising p′ at constant e always pushes ψ up, because the critical state line itself drops as stress rises. It isn't a quirk of this calculator — it's the same reason two identical soils at different depths can appear to have very different states.

Follow the chain above. Each stage answers a different question, and it's the organizing structure for Chapters 4–5. Before any formula: here's the single fact that makes a raw CPT reading useless on its own.

Particle-scale intuition

Dense of critical, ψ < 0
Grains lock tightly against the advancing cone. Tip resistance is disproportionately HIGH once normalized for stress — Qp is large.
Loose of critical, ψ > 0
Grains collapse ahead of the cone with little resistance. Tip resistance is disproportionately LOW once normalized — Qp is small.

What actually changes Qp?

Two things move Qp at a fixed qt reading, and only one of them is state. Here's what each tab means before Sections 5 and 7 make it precise:

Increase qt at fixed p′o: Qp rises directly — a genuinely stronger reading at the same stress level.
Increase p′o at fixed qt: Qp FALLS, even though nothing about the soil changed. This is exactly the depth effect in the chart above, working in reverse.
Decrease ψ (denser): Qp rises exponentially, per Qp=k·exp(−mψ) — this is the actual relationship Section 7 inverts.
Sign-convention checkpoint: this module keeps Module 1's convention — ψ<0 is dense/dilative/strong, ψ>0 is loose/contractive/weak.

5. From raw CPT readings to dimensionless resistance

A raw CPT gives three channels: tip resistance qt, sleeve friction fs, and pore pressure u2. As Section 3's demo just showed, none of these mean anything on their own — resistance scales almost linearly with stress level, so the book's Table 4.1 strips that out with dimensionless groups:

Q = (qt − σvo) / σ′vo    F = fs/(qt − σvo)    Bq = (u2 − u0)/(qt − σvo)
p′o = σ′vo(1+2Ko)/3    Qp = (qt − p′o) / p′o    (≈ 3Q/(1+2Ko), Table 4.1)

Bq should stay within about ±0.02 for the drained-penetration relationships used below (Section 4.7.3) — the badge flags it if not.

qt (raw, MPa)Q (dimensionless)Qp (dimensionless)

Where does Ko actually come from? Section 4.9.1's answer: be skeptical

The formula everyone reaches for is Jaky's equation, linking Ko to the current friction angle:

Ko = 1 − sinφ′   (Eq. 4.19)
Ko = 1 − sinφ′0.46
The book is explicitly skeptical of this formula here, not just presenting it: densifying a sand by vibration raises its friction angle, so Eq. 4.19 predicts Ko should fall — but real measurements show horizontal stress increases with densification. "Equation 4.19 has the wrong sense." The book's actual conclusion: at best, Jaky's equation just indicates Ko ≈ 0.5 under loose, normally consolidated conditions — treat it as a rough anchor, not a real physical relationship.

7. Inverting for ψ: the dimensionless CPT method

The book's core result (Eqs. 4.7/4.8, from Been et al. 1986/87's calibration chamber database) is a simple exponential relationship between Qp and ψ, with the two fitting constants k and m predictable directly from a sand's CSL slope λ10 (Eqs. 4.11a/b) — the same λ10 already in Module 1's Table 2.1 database:

Qp = k·exp(−mψ)    ψ = −ln(Qp/k)/m
k = 8 + 0.55/(λ10 − 0.01)    m = 8.1 − 2.3·log10(λ10)   (Eqs. 4.11a, 4.11b)

This chapter's method recovers ψ to about ±0.05 against the calibration-chamber database using λ10 alone — but Section 4.6.2–4.6.4 shows it's biased with stress level because it ignores elastic rigidity Ir=G/p′. The book's own example: for Ticino sand at Qp=100, the overall trend gives ψ=−0.14, but splitting the same data by stress level gives ψ≈−0.19 at p′=400 kPa versus ψ≈−0.10 at p′=40 kPa — a real Δψ≈0.05 hidden inside the "simple" fit. Shuttle & Jefferies (1998) traced this to Ir, then to NorSand's own H, M, N, λ (in roughly that order of importance, per Fig. 4.25) — the fully-corrected inversion (Eq. 4.12, Table 4.3) removes the bias but needs those extra properties, so it isn't reproduced here as a calculator (see disclosure on the last page).

8. How variable is ψ really? Choosing a characteristic value ψk

Chapter 5's central point: ψ measured continuously down a real CPT sounding is never one number, it's a distribution — and Rowe & Craig's centrifuge caisson tests found that as little as 4% loose material by volume measurably degraded cyclic performance, with 10% triggering outright liquefaction failure during testing. So which percentile of the distribution should a design actually use? The book's real example is the Nerlerk berm (Module 1's Ch. 1 flow-failure case history): over the 3–7 m depth interval, the median CPT-derived ψ ≈ −0.08 looked comfortably stable, but the 80th percentile was ≈ −0.03, and Onisiphorou's stochastic re-analysis found liquefiable behavior starting around ψ > −0.02 — i.e. the "average" soil looked fine while a real, connected fraction of it did not.

ψk = ψμ + z(percentile)·ψσ   (assuming a normal distribution of ψ; z from the inverse standard normal CDF)

Defaults are the book's own Nerlerk berm stochastic-analysis input (ψμ=−0.08, ψσ=0.05, Onisiphorou 2000). "80th percentile" here follows the book's convention: the value that 80% of measurements are stronger (denser) than — i.e. the 80th percentile of the ψ distribution itself, since higher ψ is weaker. Practical guidance from Section 5.5.1: 80th–90th percentile, erring toward 90th for cyclic loading and relaxing toward 80th for static problems.
