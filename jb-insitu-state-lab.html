About this module

Module 1 built the mathematical core — the state parameter ψ and the NorSand model — from laboratory triaxial data. But you can't sample sand undisturbed; almost everything in practice comes from a cone penetrometer (CPT) pushed into the ground. Chapter 4 solves the resulting inverse problem: given a measured tip resistance, what ψ produced it? Chapter 5 then asks the uncomfortable follow-up question every real site raises — ψ is never one number, it's a distribution, so what single value should actually go into a design calculation?

1. From raw CPT readings to dimensionless resistance

A raw CPT gives three channels: tip resistance qt, sleeve friction fs, and pore pressure u2. None of these mean anything on their own — resistance scales almost linearly with stress level, so the book's Table 4.1 strips that out with dimensionless groups:

Q = (qt − σvo) / σ′vo    F = fs/(qt − σvo)    Bq = (u2 − u0)/(qt − σvo)
p′o = σ′vo(1+2Ko)/3    Qp = (qt − p′o) / p′o    (≈ 3Q/(1+2Ko), Table 4.1)

Bq should stay within about ±0.02 for the drained-penetration relationships used below (Section 4.7.3) — the badge flags it if not.

Where does Ko actually come from? Section 4.9.1's answer: be skeptical

The formula everyone reaches for is Jaky's equation, linking Ko to the current friction angle:

Ko = 1 − sinφ′   (Eq. 4.19)
Ko = 1 − sinφ′0.46
The book is explicitly skeptical of this formula here, not just presenting it: densifying a sand by vibration raises its friction angle, so Eq. 4.19 predicts Ko should fall — but real measurements show horizontal stress increases with densification. "Equation 4.19 has the wrong sense." The book's actual conclusion: at best, Jaky's equation just indicates Ko ≈ 0.5 under loose, normally consolidated conditions — treat it as a rough anchor, not a real physical relationship.

2. Soil behaviour–type index, Ic

Before trusting a ψ estimate, the CPT itself tells you whether you're even in sand. Been & Jefferies' index uses the piezometric channel (Eq. 4.13a); Robertson & Wride's ignores it (Eq. 4.13b, and per the book, "best avoided" when pore pressure data exists):

Ic = √[(3−log(Q(1−Bq)+1))² + (1.5+1.3·log(F))²]    Ic(R&W) = √[(3.47−log(Q))² + (1.22+log(F))²]

3. Inverting for ψ: the dimensionless CPT method

The book's core result (Eqs. 4.7/4.8, from Been et al. 1986/87's calibration chamber database) is a simple exponential relationship between Qp and ψ, with the two fitting constants k and m predictable directly from a sand's CSL slope λ10 (Eqs. 4.11a/b) — the same λ10 already in the Table 2.1 database from Module 1:

Qp = k·exp(−mψ)    ψ = −ln(Qp/k)/m
k = 8 + 0.55/(λ10 − 0.01)    m = 8.1 − 2.3·log10(λ10)   (Eqs. 4.11a, 4.11b)

This chapter's method recovers ψ to about ±0.05 against the calibration-chamber database using λ10 alone — but Section 4.6.2–4.6.4 shows it's biased with stress level because it ignores elastic rigidity Ir=G/p′. The book's own example: for Ticino sand at Qp=100, the overall trend gives ψ=−0.14, but splitting the same data by stress level gives ψ≈−0.19 at p′=400 kPa versus ψ≈−0.10 at p′=40 kPa — a real Δψ≈0.05 hidden inside the "simple" fit. Shuttle & Jefferies (1998) traced this to Ir, then to NorSand's own H, M, N, λ (in roughly that order of importance, per Fig. 4.25) — the fully-corrected inversion (Eq. 4.12, Table 4.3) removes the bias but needs those extra properties, so it isn't reproduced here as a calculator (see disclosure below).

4. How variable is ψ really? Choosing a characteristic value ψk

Chapter 5's central point: ψ measured continuously down a real CPT sounding is never one number, it's a distribution — and Rowe & Craig's centrifuge caisson tests found that as little as 4% loose material by volume measurably degraded cyclic performance, with 10% triggering outright liquefaction failure during testing. So which percentile of the distribution should a design actually use? The book's real example is the Nerlerk berm (Ch. 1's flow-failure case history): over the 3–7 m depth interval, the median CPT-derived ψ ≈ −0.08 looked comfortably stable, but the 80th percentile was ≈ −0.03, and Onisiphorou's stochastic re-analysis found liquefiable behavior starting around ψ > −0.02 — i.e. the "average" soil looked fine while a real, connected fraction of it did not.

ψk = ψμ + z(percentile)·ψσ   (assuming a normal distribution of ψ; z from the inverse standard normal CDF)

Defaults are the book's own Nerlerk berm stochastic-analysis input (ψμ=−0.08, ψσ=0.05, Onisiphorou 2000). "80th percentile" here follows the book's convention: the value that 80% of measurements are stronger (denser) than — i.e. the 80th percentile of the ψ distribution itself, since higher ψ is weaker. Practical guidance from Section 5.5.1: 80th–90th percentile, erring toward 90th for cyclic loading and relaxing toward 80th for static problems.

About this tool. Section 1's dimensionless groups and Section 2's Ic formulas (Eqs. 4.13a/b, Table 4.4 boundaries) are transcribed exactly. Section 3's Qp–ψ relationship (Eqs. 4.7, 4.8, 4.11a, 4.11b) is the book's own λ10-only calibration and is presented together with its own documented limitation (stress-level/rigidity bias, ±0.05, Section 4.6.2). The book's fully rigidity-corrected general inversion (Eq. 4.12, Table 4.3) is real and cited, but was not implemented as a calculator here: the source PDF's typesetting of Eq. 4.12 could not be extracted unambiguously (an exponent/constant of 1.45 appears in a position consistent with either k1.45=∏fi or a scaling constant, and this could not be confirmed against the original 1998 paper), so rather than guess, the qualitative finding (H and M matter most, N and λ less, ν negligible) is reported without a fabricated formula. Section 4's characteristic-value calculator assumes a normal distribution of ψ for illustration; the book notes real sand strength distributions often skew log-normal.
