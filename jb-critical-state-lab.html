1. From “dense or loose?” to a computable model of soil response

This module builds the one mental model needed for the rest of the book: compare the current soil state with the critical state at the same mean effective stress. The distance between them, ψ, organizes contraction, dilation, peak strength, and the direction of the stress–strain response.

Recommended route: first use the visual mental model, then the guided CSL tool, then stress–dilatancy, and only then open the NorSand simulator.
The big idea
ψ = e − ec(p′)

Void ratio alone is not enough. Stress alone is not enough. ψ combines both by measuring vertical distance from the critical state line.

About this module

This book builds its entire treatment of liquefaction — static and cyclic, laboratory and in-situ, case histories and constitutive models — on one idea: a soil's behavior is governed not just by its density or its stress level alone, but by how far it sits from the critical state, the unique void ratio a soil settles into after enough shearing at a given effective stress. That distance is the state parameter, ψ. Everything downstream — whether a sand contracts or dilates, how strong it is, whether it can flow-fail — is, to first order, a function of ψ and a small number of soil properties.

This first module works through that mathematical core using the book's own real, published data: the critical state properties of 17 sands and tailings from Table 2.11, and the NorSand constitutive model derived in Chapter 3. From here, Module 2 covers in-situ determination of ψ from CPT data (Chapters 4–5), and Module 3 and Module 4 cover the static and cyclic liquefaction triggering framework (Chapters 6, 7 & 9) that this all leads to.

4. The critical state line & the state parameter ψ

Every real sand has its own critical state line (CSL) — the void ratio it reaches at large shear strain, as a function of confining stress — commonly idealized as semi-log:

ec = Γ − λ·ln(p′)   (Eq. 2.1 exactly as printed — natural log)

The book is explicit that both forms are in everyday use, and gives the exact conversion: natural logs (λ) are more convenient for constitutive modelling, base-10 logs (λ10) arise naturally when plotting lab data by hand — λ10 = 2.303·λ. Table 2.1 and the rest of this page use λ10, so here is the same line written that way — algebraically identical, just a different log base:

ec = Γ − λ10·log10(p′)   (identical line, λ10-form — used throughout this page)

A curved alternative — and the book has a real example. The semi-log form above is a convenient idealization, and the book is explicit that it isn't the only option (a related power-law approach is also used elsewhere in the literature, e.g. Li & Wang (1998)*). For Toyoura sand — one of the 17 sands in this page's own Table 2.1 data — the book gives its own power-law fit, Eq. 2.12, that straightens the curvature its Fig. 2.29 shows in semi-log space:

ec = 0.9325 − 0.19·(p′/pref)0.7,   pref = 100 kPa

In the general form ec = eΓ − λc·(p′/pref)ξ, the three fitted parameters — eΓ (intercept), λc (coefficient), and the exponent ξ — play the same general role as Γ and λ10 above, but the numbers above are Toyoura's own real, published values (Eq. 2.12, fitted to Verdugo (1992) data) — not invented for illustration.

* Li, X.S. & Wang, Y. (1998). "Linear representation of steady-state line for sand." Journal of Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Engineering, ASCE, 124(12), 1215–1217.

The state parameter is simply the current void ratio's distance from that line at the current stress:

ψ = e − ec   (Eq. 2.6) — ψ < 0: denser than critical (dilative) · ψ > 0: looser than critical (contractive)

5. Stress–dilatancy: how ψ sets strength

Dilatancy, D, is the ratio of the volumetric strain increment to the shear (deviatoric) strain increment, D = dεv/dεq (Eq. 2.4) — how much a soil is bulking up or compacting for a given amount of shearing. Under the book's compression-positive sign convention, D is negative while a sample is dilating, so its most negative value during a test — usually reached at peak strength — is written Dmin. Dilation at peak strength scales linearly with ψ through a soil property χtc (fit from the same triaxial database):

Dmin = χtc·ψ   (Eq. 2.11)

One more property is needed before going further: Mtc, the critical stress ratio η = q/p′ a soil in triaxial compression heads toward at large strain — the same flat target every sample converges to eventually, whatever ψ it started at (that's the whole point of "critical"). It's a property of the sand itself, not of its current density, and you'll see exactly how it's measured in Section 6. Rowe/Nova's stress–dilatancy rule2 (D = M − η) then converts dilation directly into a peak stress ratio above (or below) that critical value Mtc, which converts to a peak friction angle via the standard triaxial Mohr–Coulomb relation sinφ′ = 3η/(6+η):

2. Rowe's 1962 original relates mobilized friction to dilation through a more elaborate ratio derived from a sliding-block energy analysis. Nova's rule, as first incorporated into NorSand (Eq. 3.31), is actually the fuller form with the (1−N) term shown above — it's the simple D = M − η form used throughout most of this page (Eq. 3.32) that came later, from a further Dafalias-inspired revision, not from Nova's own simplification.
ηmax = Mtc − Dmin    sinφ′peak = 3ηmax / (6 + ηmax)

6. Where do Γ, λ10, Mtc, χtc, N and H actually come from?

NorSand's six soil properties aren't looked up in a table — every one of them is fit to a plot of laboratory test results. The panels below use the sand currently selected in Section 4 (Erksak 330/0.7 sand) and show, for each parameter, an illustrative set of test points scattered around that sand's real published value, then fit a line or curve through them right in front of you — the same logic the book uses on real data (Sections 2.4, 2.6, 3.7). The scatter points are synthetic (generated to plausibly resemble real test data), but the target value each one is built around is the real number from Table 2.1 — see the disclosure at the bottom of this page.

Even the book's own numbers aren't perfectly unique. Section 2.5, "Uniqueness of the CSL," is upfront that repeat calibrations of the same sand rarely land on identical properties. Erksak 330/0.7 is the book's own example, and it shows disagreement at three separate levels. Table 2.1 itself (the general sand-property database, Chapter 2) lists Mtc=1.27 for this sand. Chapter 3's validation (Section 3.7.1) instead uses Mtc=1.26, Γ=0.816, χtc=3.5 — a recalibration one hundredth off Table 2.1's own number. Chapter 6's flow-liquefaction case study recalibrates the same sand again, to Γ=0.817, χtc=3.8 (keeping Mtc=1.26 and λ10=0.031). This page uses the Chapter 3 values throughout. Every one of these gaps is small, but together they're a real, book-documented illustration that "critical state properties" are a best-fit from a finite test program — even within one book, about one sand — not a physical constant read off nature.3
3. The book also documents an alternative image stress-ratio rule, Mi = M(1−|ψi|/Mtc) (Jefferies & Shuttle 2002), noting it matches NorSand's own rule (used throughout this page) on average at peak dilation — close, but not identical, another small reminder that even the model's own internal relationships aren't perfectly unique.

Rowe/Nova's stress–dilatancy rule in its full form is η = Mi − (1−N)·Dp (NorSand's Eq. 3.31) — a straight line of slope N−1 on a D–η plot, not exactly −1. This is genuinely the method Fig. 2.30 uses on real Erksak data: the book's own trend line there is explicitly labeled with slope N−1, so that single plot calibrates both Mtc (the intercept) and N (the slope) together. At the special point D=0 (no volume change), the η-intercept is Mtc regardless of the slope, since the (1−N)·D term vanishes — that part of the logic is unaffected by which form you use. This single instant isn't itself critical state (which needs continued deformation at constant stress and constant void ratio, not just one point of zero dilation); it's simply the stress ratio momentarily matching the value Mtc that critical state settles at, which is exactly why D=0 is the intercept used to read Mtc off this plot. The simpler D=M−η form used elsewhere on this page (slope exactly −1, i.e. N=0) is a common first-order simplification: exact at D=0, but not the book's own full relationship away from that point.

7. NorSand: simulating a drained triaxial compression test

Chapter 3 develops NorSand, the constitutive model that turns ψ from a static index into an evolving internal state variable capable of predicting an entire stress–strain curve, not just its peak. The core idea is a yield surface — a boundary in stress space that separates the elastic and plastic response, whose current size is tracked by the internal variable pi. As the sample strains, this surface grows or shrinks toward an "image" condition set by how far the soil sits from critical state; that evolving size, not a fixed strength value, is what produces the whole stress–strain curve below. Below is a direct numerical integration of NorSand's own governing equations (Table 3.1) for a drained triaxial compression test at constant mean effective stress — the same idealized state path the book itself uses in Figure 2.5 to introduce ψ. Two simplifications are made explicit in full below the results: elastic strains are neglected (the book notes this is standard practice when reducing stress–dilatancy data), and the simpler difference-form hardening law (Eq. 3.39) is used in place of its dimensionless reformulation (Eq. 3.40).

The seventh (and eighth) properties: elasticity, Ir and ν

NorSand needs seven properties in total — the six above plus a Poisson's ratio ν. Elasticity itself is set by a single dimensionless number, the rigidity index Ir = G/p′ (elastic shear modulus over mean stress, Table 3.2's typical range 100–600 for sands): high Ir means the soil is very stiff relative to how hard it's being confined (dense, heavily overconsolidated), low Ir means it's compliant relative to its confinement (loose, lightly stressed). A complete implementation uses Ir and ν to compute an elastic response inside the yield surface, before any plastic correction; this simulator neglects elastic strains entirely instead (see "What this simulator actually does" below the governing equations), so Ir plays no role in the curves above — but you can still see what it represents:

Governing equations used (all from Table 3.1 and Eqs. 3.31–3.39)4

4. Table 3.1's full critical-stress-ratio expression also carries a Lode-angle dependence for general (non-triaxial-compression) stress states: M = Mtc − (Mtc2/(3+Mtc))·cos(3θ/2 + π/4). This simulator is restricted to triaxial compression, where that expression reduces exactly to M = Mtc — which is why it doesn't appear below.

These seven equations aren't independent facts to memorize — they're one computational loop, repeated every strain increment. Each line feeds the next; the last line feeds back into the first for the next step:

1 · How far the internal yield surface currently is from critical
ψi = ψ − λ·ln(p′/pi)

Uses the internal variable pi, not p′ directly — this is what lets the yield surface evolve independently of the real stress path.

↓
2 · A fixed property of the sand, rescaled once (not every step)
χi = χtc / (1 − χtcλ/Mtc)

Converts χtc (fit in Section 6) into its "image" form — a constant for this sand, computed once at the start.

↓
3 · The model's evolving target
Mi = Mtc − χiN|ψi|

χi and the current ψi from step 1 combine to set the image stress ratio — how far the yield surface's target currently sits from Mtc.

↓
4 · The outer NorSand yield locus in triaxial p′–q space
η = q/p′ = Mi·(1 − ln(p′/pi))  ⇔  q = Mi·p′·(1 − ln(p′/pi))

At the current pi and p′, this is the stress ratio this locus predicts this instant. Two landmarks on the curve: at the image condition p′=pi, ln(p′/pi)=0 so η=Mi exactly (q=Mi·pi) — not the same thing as critical state, which needs continued plastic shearing with no further change in stress ratio or void ratio, and Mi is generally still moving toward Mtc at that point. At the isotropic intercept q=0, ln(p′/pi)=1 so p′=exp(1)·pi (≈2.718·pi — Euler's number, not void ratio e). This is the triaxial-compression meridional section of NorSand's yield surface; the full three-dimensional surface also depends on the Lode angle through the applicable critical stress ratio (footnote 4 above).

↓
5 · The gap between "heading toward" and "is" becomes motion
Dp = Mi − η

This is the actual plastic dilation rate for this increment — the same D = M − η rule from Section 5, now evaluated with the image values. It's what moves e.

↓
6 · Where pi itself is drifting toward
pi,max = p′·exp(−χiψi/Mtc)

ψi sets the size the yield surface is trying to grow (or shrink) toward. This step uses the fixed Mtc, not the evolving Mi from step 3 — Eq. 3.38/Table 3.1's difference-form hardening law is written with the reference Mitc, which equals Mtc for the triaxial-compression path this tool models. Using the evolving Mi here would make pi,max circularly depend on itself through ψi.

↓
7 · How fast it actually gets there — then repeat from step 1
Δpi = H·(pi,max − pi)·Δεq

H sets how quickly pi catches up to pi,max each increment. Advance pi by this amount, then go back to step 1 for the next slice of strain.

Yield locus vs. stress path: the yield locus (step 4) is the boundary of stress states the current internal variables pi and Mi allow without further plastic strain — a snapshot of how big the surface is right now. The stress path is the route the actual (p′,η) point follows as strain accumulates. Hardening or softening doesn't move the stress point off some fixed shape; it changes the locus itself, strain by strain, as pi and Mi evolve through steps 1–3 and 6–7. A single stress state sitting on the locus is a yield point; the locus is every combination that would activate plastic loading.
What this simulator actually does, precisely. It initializes the isotropic starting stress (q=0, p′=p′0) directly on the outer yield locus — since p′0=exp(1)·pi0 is exactly that locus's own isotropic intercept (step 4), this is a property of where the starting point happens to land, not a general-purpose NorSand initialization procedure for arbitrary stress histories. From there it neglects elastic strains, treats the imposed shear-strain increment as if it were entirely plastic, and evolves pi directly through the simplified hardening law above (steps 6–7) — without computing the elastic trial stress and plastic return-mapping step a complete implementation performs every increment once the trial stress reaches the surface: inside the locus a stress increment is elastic; once a trial stress reaches or exceeds it, plastic correction and hardening take over. Real NorSand loading doesn't generally begin directly on the yield surface the way this simulator's idealized path does. In short, this is a plastic-on-surface educational integration — useful for building intuition about what each parameter does to the shape of the curve, not a full elastoplastic stress integration, and its curves should be read as conceptual demonstrations of plastic state evolution under one idealized stress path. A production implementation — for example in FLAC3D — additionally carries stress-dependent elastic shear and bulk moduli, a genuine elastic-predictor/plastic-corrector stress return, an internal cap, and general three-dimensional stress states. Use this tool to understand parameter roles and qualitative trends; it isn't a substitute for a verified constitutive-model implementation or engineering analysis.

Section 5's formulas (Eq. 2.11, Rowe/Nova stress–dilatancy, and the standard triaxial sinφ′=3η/(6+η) conversion) are exact. Section 6's "sample triaxial test program" table is illustrative, not a real lab data sheet: the confining stresses and target ψ0 values are chosen to give a representative spread from loose to dense, and every other column (e0, peak η, φ′peak, Dmin) is then computed exactly from this sand's real Table 2.1 properties using the same equations shown throughout this page — it's meant to show the shape and structure of real triaxial data, not to reproduce any specific test. The "one test, start to finish" pipeline directly beneath the table works the same way: the "raw" q and εv curves are a NorSand simulation (this sand's real properties) with pseudo-random noise added at sparse sample points, and the stress–dilatancy plot's (D, η) pairs are numerically differentiated from those noisy curves — not read off the smooth model directly — which is why the fitted Mtc doesn't land exactly on the real value, and lands closer for some tests than others: differentiating noisy data amplifies scatter, and a test whose D range doesn't reach near zero needs a longer, less reliable extrapolation to the D=0 intercept. Both effects are real phenomena in actual stress–dilatancy data reduction, not artifacts of this tool. The four panels' data points are similarly synthetic, not digitized from the book's actual figures (the book's own real test data isn't available to reproduce here): each panel generates illustrative scatter by taking this sand's real, published Table 2.1 value (or, for panel D, an illustrative N/H pair) and adding deterministic pseudo-random noise, then fits a line back through the noisy points using ordinary least squares — so the "recovered" value you see should land close to, but not necessarily exactly on, the real one, exactly as happens with real scattered lab data. The underlying relationships used (Eq. 2.1 for the CSL, Eq. 2.11 for χtc, and NorSand's own equations for the Mtc-convergence and N/H panels) are all real and exact; only the "measured" points around them are constructed for illustration. Section 7's NorSand simulator implements the model's real governing equations (Table 3.1) but makes two disclosed simplifications for a browser-friendly explicit integration: (1) the stress path is idealized as constant mean effective stress p′ (the same idealization the book itself uses in Fig. 2.5, and directly imposed here rather than solved via full elastic–plastic coupling), and (2) elastic strains are neglected so that total shear strain is treated as plastic strain (a simplification the book explicitly endorses for stress–dilatancy data reduction in Section 2.1.3). The hardening law uses the simple difference form (Eq. 3.39) rather than its dimensionless reformulation (Eq. 3.40). N and H are not given for Erksak sand in the book's worked example, so the default N=0.30 and H≈4/λ (the book's own stated first-estimate rule, Table 3.2) are used as literature-typical values. All eight sliders (Γ, λ10, Mtc, χtc, N, H, e0, p′0) are freely adjustable and independent of Section 4's sand selector, so you can explore illustrative, made-up combinations — not just the book's own published sands — to build intuition for what each one individually does to the curve; the "Erksak preset" option is just a sensible starting point, not a constraint.

9. Calibrating NorSand properly: a Bayesian worked example

Section 7's "one-variable-at-a-time" sliders are built to isolate what a single NorSand parameter does to the equations — drag one, watch the curve change, everything else held fixed. That's genuinely useful for building intuition about the mathematics. It is not a model of how a real soil's parameters behave once calibrated: in a highly non-linear model like NorSand, parameters are frequently correlated, and treating them as independently adjustable can misinform a sensitivity study.

This page works through the real alternative on real data: Contreras, Rojas-Huaroto, Halliday & Llano-Serna (2025) published a Bayesian calibration of NorSand against Fraser River sand triaxial tests, with their full Python implementation and dataset released alongside the paper. Everything below is a from-scratch port of their actual code — the forward-Euler elastoplastic model (not the simplified constant-p′ idealization used in Section 7 above) and their real Markov Chain Monte Carlo calibration (emcee's affine-invariant ensemble sampler), running entirely in this page.

Paper: Contreras, L.F., Rojas-Huaroto, H., Halliday, A. & Llano-Serna, M. (2025). Bayesian Calibration of NorSand Model Parameters Using Triaxial Test Data. Australian Geomechanics, Volume 60, No. 4, December 2025. https://doi.org/10.56295/AGJ6041

Code and data (Python/Jupyter Notebook, MCMC calibration, Fraser River sand triaxial dataset, GPLv2): github.com/LF-Contreras/Bayesian-calibration-NorSand-model

Their notebook, and the two sections below, break into the same two parts: Part 1 runs the NorSand forward model against a chosen test and set of parameters; Part 2 takes that same model and calibrates it against all 12 tests at once with Bayesian MCMC. Part 2 is the one this comment thread was actually about — Part 1 is included because Part 2 depends on it and because it's what lets you see what a given posterior sample actually predicts.

What "one point of zero dilation" doesn't mean

A related note from calibration practice, worth having alongside Section 6's stress–dilatancy fitting: the D=0 crossing used there to read off Mtc is an intercept, not a claim that the sample has reached critical state at that instant. Critical state needs continued plastic shearing with no further change in stress ratio or void ratio — a single zero-dilatancy point mid-test can occur well before that, particularly for dense samples whose D crosses zero once on the way to a peak and passes through it again on the way back down.

10. How λ10 and state build the state parameter ψ — and set strength

One equation governs it all. Compressibility (λ10), how tightly the soil is packed (e), and how hard it is squeezed (p′) combine into the state parameter ψ — the soil's distance from the critical state line. That distance, not λ10 alone, decides whether the soil is strong or weak. Move the sliders and watch it happen.

1 · State vs the CSL. The line's steepness is λ10 (compressibility). ψ is the vertical gap from your point to the line. Below the line → dense & strong; above → loose & weak.

2 · Strength follows ψ. Peak strength above critical = −k·ψ (a straight line). Your point slides along it as ψ changes — the pull λ10 and stress exert on strength runs entirely through ψ.
