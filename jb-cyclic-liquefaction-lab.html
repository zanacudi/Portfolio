About this module

Module 3 covered static liquefaction — a soil failing under its own sustained load. This module covers cyclic liquefaction: repeated earthquake loading progressively building pore pressure until strength is lost, then asks the practical question every project eventually reaches — how do you actually turn triaxial and CPT data into a defensible design number?

1. The classical framework: CRR, magnitude scaling, and two "fudge factors"

The dominant liquefaction-assessment method in practice — what the book calls the Berkeley School approach (Seed and co-workers) — is built entirely from case histories: plot earthquake-induced cyclic stress ratio (CSR = τcyc/σ′vo) against penetration resistance for sites that did and didn't liquefy, and draw a boundary (the CRR). That boundary is calibrated for M=7.5 earthquakes and near-level ground, so it needs correction factors to use elsewhere:

CRR = CRR7.5·KM·Kσ·Kα   (Eq. 7.4)

KM values are the book's tabulated magnitude scaling factors (Fig. 7.32, Seed & Idriss 1982), interpolated between the five published points.

Kσ and Kα are where the classical method gets murky. Kσ (the stress-level correction) has had wildly different recommendations from different workers (Figs. 7.36–7.37) because it's treated as a geological curve-fit rather than mechanics. Kα (the sloping-ground/static-bias correction) is worse: the book shows it depends on principal stress rotation in a way that "cannot give simple trends" — project-specific NorSand simulation is recommended instead of a formula. Section 2 shows why Kσ in particular turns out to be unnecessary.

2. The state parameter view — and why Kσ disappears

The book's real contribution here: reprocessing the same case-history database (Moss 2003's Class A records) directly in terms of ψk instead of penetration resistance produces a strikingly clean boundary — and the reason Kσ exists at all turns out to be that a soil at constant density has a different ψ at different depths (deeper = higher stress = smaller ψ, Eq. 2.1). Once you use ψ directly, that stress-level dependence is already built in and Kσ becomes unnecessary — the book shows computed Kσ-from-ψ curves overlay published empirical Kσ curves almost exactly (Fig. 7.44).

CRR7.5 = 0.06·exp(−9·ψk)   (Eq. 7.5)

3. From lab data to a design CRR curve

For a project-specific soil, Chapter 9 recommends fitting your own cyclic simple shear (CSS) test results (CSR vs. cycles-to-liquefaction NL, at several ψ) to a simple linear trend rather than an automated regression, then combining it with the magnitude scaling from Section 1:

CRR = (a − b·ψo)·KM   (Eq. 9.9, combined with the KM lookup from Section 1)

Defaults (a=0.04, b=0.8) are the book's own illustrative fit to real cyclic simple shear data at NL=15 (Fig. 9.14) — for an actual project these come from your own CSS testing, not these numbers. NL=15 ↔ M=7.5, NL=10 ↔ M=7.0, NL=6 ↔ M=6.0 (the book's guideline mapping used to select which CSS cycle-count to test at).

About this tool. Section 1's KM values are the five points tabulated in Fig. 7.32 (Seed & Idriss 1982), linearly interpolated between them — the book doesn't give a closed-form KM(M) equation, only these tabulated points, so no functional form is invented. Section 2 uses Eq. 7.5 exactly as published; the 20 real case-history points plotted are Class A records transcribed from Table 7.3 (ψk, CSR7.5, and the observed liquefaction outcome), and every point's classification was checked against Eq. 7.5's predicted CRR before plotting. Section 3's combination of Eq. 9.9 with the Section 1 KM lookup is this tool's own synthesis of two separately-presented pieces of the book (design CRR-ψ trend, and magnitude scaling) — the book doesn't state this combined formula explicitly, though both halves are exactly as published and the combination follows directly from Eq. 7.4's structure. Kσ and Kα are deliberately not implemented as calculators: the book shows Kσ is redundant once working in ψ (Section 2), and states outright that Kα "cannot give simple trends" without project-specific NorSand modelling.
