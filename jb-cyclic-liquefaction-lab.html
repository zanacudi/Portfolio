1. The same soil, a different question

Module 3 asked what happens when a contractive soil fails under its own sustained load. This module asks what happens when the load arrives as a few dozen cycles of earthquake shaking instead — and then asks the practical question every project eventually reaches: how do you turn laboratory and CPT data into a design number you can defend?

The answer the profession actually uses was built entirely from case histories, and it works. The book's contribution is to show why it works, and that one of its two correction factors is an artefact of using penetration resistance rather than state. Once you work in ψ, that factor simply stops being needed.

What you will be able to do by the end. Explain the difference between cyclic mobility and flow liquefaction, and why one is survivable. Apply the classical CRR framework with magnitude scaling. Show, from the book's own case-history database, that a ψ-based boundary separates 19 of 20 real records. Explain why Kσ is redundant and why Kα resists a formula. Fit a project-specific design curve from cyclic simple shear data.

2. Cyclic mobility is not flow liquefaction

Two things get called liquefaction and they behave completely differently. In flow liquefaction — Module 3's subject — a contractive soil loses strength and cannot get it back, so once it starts moving the driving stress exceeds the residual strength and it keeps going. In cyclic mobility, pore pressure ratchets up cycle by cycle and strains accumulate, but the soil is dilative enough that each cycle recovers some stiffness. It deforms, sometimes badly. It does not run.

The distinction is the state parameter, again. Drag ψ below the dilative boundary and the same cyclic loading produces large but self-limiting strains; drag it above and the soil has somewhere to fall to.

What is real here is the distinction: whether reaching ru=1 means large recoverable strains or a runaway, and that is decided by ψ, not by the number of cycles.

3. The classical framework, and its two correction factors

The dominant method in practice — what the book calls the Berkeley School approach, after Seed and co-workers — is built entirely from case histories. Plot the earthquake-induced cyclic stress ratio against penetration resistance for sites that did and did not liquefy, and draw a boundary. That boundary is the cyclic resistance ratio, CRR. It is calibrated for magnitude 7.5 earthquakes on near-level ground, so using it anywhere else needs corrections:

CRR = CRR7.5 · KM · Kσ · Kα   (Eq. 7.4)
KM comes from the five points tabulated in Fig. 7.32 (Seed & Idriss 1982), interpolated between them. The book gives no closed form, so none is invented here.
A smaller earthquake shakes fewer times, so the soil survives a larger stress ratio: KM is above 1 below magnitude 7.5 and below 1 above it.
KM interpolatedthe five tabulated pointsyour design magnitude
Where the classical method gets murky. Kσ, the stress-level correction, has had wildly different recommendations from different workers (Figs. 7.36–7.37), because it is treated as a curve fit to geology rather than as mechanics. Kα, the sloping-ground correction, is worse: the book shows it depends on principal stress rotation in a way that “cannot give simple trends”. Sections 6 and 7 take these two in turn — the first turns out to be unnecessary, the second turns out to be genuinely hard.

5. The state parameter view of the same database

Here is the book's real contribution to this chapter. Take the same case-history database — Moss (2003)'s Class A records — and reprocess it in terms of ψk instead of penetration resistance. The boundary that emerges is strikingly clean, and it is a single exponential with no correction factors attached:

6. Watch Kσ disappear

Why does the classical method need a stress-level correction at all? Because penetration resistance is not state. Take one soil at one relative density and put it at two depths: it is the same soil, but the critical state line falls with stress, so the deeper element — at the same void ratio — sits closer to critical and its ψ is larger, meaning relatively looser. It therefore resists cyclic loading less, and the classical method patches that with Kσ.

Work in ψ and the correction is already inside the number. Compute ψ at each depth from the critical state line — the same Table 2.1 database Module 1 used — and feed both straight into Eq. 7.5. The ratio of the two resistances is Kσ, and it came out of the mechanics rather than a curve fit.

The shallow element is held at p′ = 100 kPa, the reference stress the classical charts are normalised to.
Kσ computed from ψrange of published empirical Kσ curvesyour deeper element
Why this matters beyond tidiness. Kσ was never a property of the soil — it is the shadow that using penetration resistance instead of state casts on the analysis. The published empirical curves disagree with each other (Figs. 7.36–7.37) precisely because each was fitted to a different set of soils at different depths, all trying to correct for something that a state-based analysis never introduces. The shaded band shows roughly where those published curves sit; the computed line is what the mechanics gives.

7. Kα, and why the book refuses to give you a formula

Kα corrects for static shear bias — ground that is already carrying shear stress before the earthquake arrives, as any slope is. It would be convenient if a static bias always reduced cyclic resistance, or always raised it. It does neither, and that is the problem.

On dense, dilative ground a static bias means the soil is already partway up its stress path in the direction it will be pushed, and cyclic resistance rises. On loose, contractive ground the same bias means each cycle drives it further toward a state it cannot recover from, and resistance falls. The sign of the correction depends on the state, and its magnitude depends on principal stress rotation, which the book shows “cannot give simple trends”.

dilative: bias helpscontractive: bias hurtsψ = −0.05 boundaryyour state and bias
This figure is a direction, not a design value. It shows the sign of the Kα effect and how it flips across the dilative/contractive boundary, which is the book's actual point. It is not a calibrated Kα curve, and it should not be used as one. The book's own recommendation for a real project is explicit: run project-specific NorSand simulations rather than reach for a factor, because principal stress rotation makes the trend genuinely soil- and path-dependent. That recommendation is the reason no Kα calculator is offered here.

8. From laboratory data to a design curve

Chapter 9's practical advice: for a project-specific soil, fit your own cyclic simple shear results — CSR against cycles to liquefaction, at several ψ — to a simple linear trend rather than an automated regression, then apply the magnitude scaling from Section 3.

CRR = (a − b·ψo) · KM   (Eq. 9.9, combined with the KM lookup from Section 3)

Where the defaults come from, and where yours should. a=0.04 and b=0.8 are the book's own illustrative fit to real cyclic simple shear data at NL=15 (Fig. 9.14). For an actual project these come from your own testing, not from these numbers. The book's guideline mapping for choosing the cycle count to test at: NL=15 for M=7.5, NL=10 for M=7.0, NL=6 for M=6.0.
