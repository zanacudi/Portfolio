1. The chapter the book has been building toward

Modules 1 and 2 were about measuring state. This one is about what state does. A contractive soil sheared undrained does not simply reach a lower strength — it reaches a peak, then sheds most of that strength on the way to critical state, and it is that drop, not the final value, that turns a slope into a flowslide.

Everything here runs on the ψ you learned to estimate in Module 2. Where Module 2 ended by asking which characteristic ψk to carry forward, this module answers what that number buys you: a residual strength, a brittleness, and a judgement about whether the ground is dense enough to leave alone.

What you will be able to do by the end. Read a flow failure and say which of its features were predictable. Compute residual strength from ψ and say how brittle the soil is. Run NorSand's own equations for an undrained test and watch liquefaction happen with no collapse surface anywhere in them. Explain why the instability line is an outcome rather than a property, and why a slope can fail with no new load at all. Run the book's published design function, check it against eleven real failures, and say how dense is dense enough — and why the older steady-state method reads too high.

2. Four flow failures, and what they have in common

Static liquefaction is not a laboratory curiosity. The book opens with real failures, and it is worth sitting with them before any equations: two happened under static loading alone, two were triggered by something else and then ran as flowslides. Select one to see what it cost and what the book draws from it.

The pattern worth noticing. Volume and death toll are barely related. Aberfan killed 144 people with roughly 107,000 m³ — about a seventieth of Fort Peck's volume, which killed eight. What decides the harm is not how much moved but how far it ran and what was in the way, and runout is governed by the residual strength this module computes.
All four share one thing. In every case the material was loose enough to be contractive at its in-situ stress, and in every case the failure ran further than a drained analysis would predict. That is the whole subject of Chapter 6: not whether the soil is strong enough to stand, but how much strength survives once it starts to move.

3. Peak strength, residual strength, and why brittleness is the real danger

The peak undrained strength of even very loose liquefiable sand is not unusually low — typically su/p′o ≈ 0.15–0.30 (Fig. 6.10), comparable to normally consolidated clay. What makes static liquefaction dangerous is what happens after the peak: a large, brittle drop to the residual (critical-state) strength. That residual follows directly from the definition of the CSL, so it is not an empirical fit — it is a consequence of where the soil started relative to critical:

sr/p′o = (Mtc/2)·exp(−ψo/λe)   (Eq. 6.4)     IB = (su−sr)/su   (Bishop's brittleness index, Eq. 6.5)
Typical range 0.15–0.30 (Fig. 6.10). Eq. 6.4 predicts the residual, not the peak, so the peak is set directly here.
sr/p′o from Eq. 6.4the brittleness gappeak su/p′ocurrent stateψo=0.7λe brittleness onset
The reference point. Test G609 on Erksak 330/0.7 sand — the loosest clean-sand liquefaction test in the book's database (eo=0.800, p′o=500 kPa, ψo=+0.068) — is the default slider state. It showed an 80% peak-to-residual strength drop, with stress ratio and pore pressure both mobilising smoothly through the whole test. No sudden collapse. That smoothness is the book's key evidence against the traditional picture, which is Section 6.

Where these numbers come from: the trends in the laboratory data

The equation above is not fitted to anything — it falls out of the definition of the critical state line. What the laboratory data supply is the other half of the picture: how high the peak sits, and therefore how big the drop is. Plotting both against ψo/λe rather than ψo alone (Fig. 6.13) is what makes the two comparable, because it is that ratio, not ψo, that governs brittleness.

Two reference points anchor the chart. Wroth's (1984) critical-state result for a normally consolidated clay, (su/p′o)nc = (M/2)(1/2)Λ with Λ≈0.8 (Eq. 6.3), gives about 0.35 at M=1.25 — and a spacing ratio of two is the same thing as ψo = 0.69λe. That is why the book can say there is no substantive difference between the peak undrained strength of loose sand and of normally consolidated clay, and why brittleness only begins at ψo/λe > 0.7 (Fig. 6.14): below it, peak and residual are the same number.

5. What undrained shearing actually does to a contractive soil

Before arguing about mechanisms it helps to watch the process. Shear a contractive soil with no drainage and it wants to contract but cannot, so the pore pressure rises and the mean effective stress falls. The stress path climbs, turns over at a peak, and then walks down the critical state line to the residual. The peak is not a material constant — it is where the path happens to turn.

effective stress pathcritical state line, q = M·p′peak (instability)residual at critical statethe strength that is lost
Read the shape, not just the numbers. Drag ψo from positive to negative and the whole character changes: a contractive soil turns over early and ends far to the left, while a dilative one climbs alongside the critical state line and ends stronger than it started. The peak stops being a peak. That transition — not a threshold stress ratio — is what separates a flowslide from a slump. The path here is computed, by the same NorSand integration Section 6 opens up, with the moduli held at representative values (H = 54, Ir = 150, χtc = 3.8, N = 0.30) so that this section stays about the shape of the mechanism; Section 6 puts those moduli on sliders and shows how much they matter. The critical-state point is marked from Eq. 6.4 rather than from the run, because a very loose sample needs more strain to reach it than a test window shows.

6. Undrained NorSand: the mechanism, computed

Section 5 drew the stress path by hand from the critical-state relationships. This section computes it. Below is a direct numerical integration of NorSand's own governing equations (Table 3.1) for an undrained triaxial compression test — the same calculation the book's downloadable NorSandM.xls performs in its NorTxlU routine, and the one Section 6.5 uses to answer the question the collapse-surface debate could not: if liquefaction is not a fabric collapse, what is it?

The undrained condition is not a soil property but a boundary condition (Eq. 6.6): total volumetric strain is zero, so the plastic contraction the soil wants must be cancelled by an equal elastic expansion, and that costs mean effective stress (Eq. 6.7):

ε̇v = 0  ⇒  ε̇ve = −ε̇vp   (6.6)     ṗ′ = −K·ε̇vp   (6.7)     with   Dp = Mi − η   and   K = 2G(1+ν)/[3(1−2ν)],   G = Irp′

That is the whole mechanism. A contractive soil (Dp > 0) destroys its own mean effective stress as it shears, while the yield surface hardens only as fast as the hardening law allows. Liquefaction is what happens when the first process outruns the second — nothing collapses, and no trigger stress ratio appears anywhere in the equations.

7. Why “collapse surface” is the wrong picture

The traditional explanation for static liquefaction is a “collapse surface” or “instability line”: loose sand supposedly holds together in a metastable particle arrangement until a critical stress ratio triggers a sudden rearrangement. The book tested that directly against Test G609. Stress ratio and excess pore pressure both mobilise smoothly with strain, with no inflection at peak strength (ηL=0.62) that would mark a structural event. A companion drained test on an even looser sample (e=0.820) showed no change in behaviour at all when its stress path crossed the same ratio.

NorSand simulations show why the idea misleads: the stress ratio at instability is not a soil property. Holding Mtc fixed and varying only the ratio of plastic hardening modulus to elastic rigidity, H/Ir, moved ηL from 0.75 to 1.0 in simulations of real Guindon silty sand (33% fines, ψo=+0.065, Fig. 6.27). Liquefaction is ordinary strain-softening plasticity that becomes catastrophic only when drainage cannot keep up with pore pressure generation — Drucker's instability postulate (Eq. 6.20).

The same range Fig. 6.27 explores, for the book's own Guindon silty sand parameters (Γ=0.902, λ10=0.05, Mtc=1.25, N=0.5, χtc=6.0, ψo=+0.065, OCR=1.15, Ko=1.0, ν=0.2).
If ηL were a soil property it could not move at all while the soil stays the same. Every point on this chart is the same silty sand at the same state. That is the argument. Note also that the two curves fall almost on top of each other — varying H and varying Ir give the same answer at the same ratio, which is why the book plots against H/Ir in the first place.

Where an “instability line” actually comes from (Fig. 6.25)

NorSand has no instability line in its formulation, yet one appears anyway. Shear the same soil undrained from different Ko conditions — that is, with different amounts of shear stress already locked in before drainage stops — and the locus through the peaks is close to a straight line through the origin. The book's own reading: instability is caused by drainage conditions, not by metastable collapse, and the line moves with state and with the moduli, so it is not a soil property either. Note what happens as Ko falls: the peak strength rises, because less of the path is in the weaker undrained mode — until the starting stress ratio is already above the locus, at which point the soil sheds strength the instant drainage stops.

8. Failure with no new load at all

Every test so far raised the stress ratio η = q/p′ by raising q. That is how laboratory shearing works, and it is most of what soil mechanics teaches. But η can just as easily be raised by lowering p′ — water infiltrating a slope, a rising water table, a blocked drain. The shear stress never changes. Nothing is built on top. The soil simply runs out of effective stress.

This was the trigger at Aberfan (Section 6.6.5). The tip was inspected the day before the failure and showed no tension cracks, no bulging, nothing. Sasitharan's laboratory version of the same path collapsed at an axial strain of 0.4% — and with such violence that, in the authors' words, the whole laboratory felt the loading head hit its stops. Constant-shear-drained tests by Junaideen and others, and the book's own test on Fraser River sand, all show the same sequence: an elastic limit well below M, then yielding, then a limiting stress ratio the equipment can no longer hold.

ηo = 3(1−Ko)/(1+2Ko). A slope carries shear stress before anything happens to it; flat ground does not.

11. Why the older steady-state method reads too high

Before the state-parameter framework, residual strength was measured directly: take undisturbed samples, test them undrained, correct the measured strength for the difference between the sample's void ratio and the in-situ void ratio, and read off a minimum assured strength. It is an appealing method, and the book spends Section 6.8 taking it apart. Every one of its shortcomings pushes the answer the same way — towards too much strength.

The correction itself is the weak point — Section 6.8.3
Residual strength depends on void ratio through an exponential, so the correction from the sample's void ratio to the in-situ one amplifies every error in either number. The calculator below is that exponential, and nothing else.
Reducing the measured strength by 30% — the “failure plane” correction
The steady-state procedure multiplies laboratory strengths by roughly cosφ′ to get the stress on the failure plane. But the limit equilibrium analysis it feeds works in (σ′1−σ′3)/2 and does not know the strength has been adjusted. The book's verdict is unusually blunt: the cosine factor is “misguided and incorrect”.
Blending samples changes the soil — the CSL is silt-sensitive
Compositing several samples to get enough material for testing produces a gradation that may exist nowhere in the deposit, and Chapter 2 shows the critical state line moves with even small changes in silt content. A single λ for a whole stratum is generally wrong.
Triaxial M is not field M — about 5%
Most real failures are close to plane strain, where the critical friction ratio differs from its triaxial compression value; the book's own simulations put the effect at roughly 5% on field strength (Fig. 6.38).
“Minimum assured strength” is a theoretical error — unless the soil is totally contractive
Only soils looser than about ψ > +0.03 are contractive all the way to critical state. The troublesome range is −0.1 < ψ < 0: lightly dilatant soils, which contract first and dilate afterwards, and for which a single minimum strength does not exist.
Undrained is an assumption, not a fact — minutes matter
In a large hydraulic fill of Erksak sand with 15 m of double drainage, 20% of the excess pore pressure was gone two minutes after cycling stopped and it had dissipated completely in about eight. Lower San Fernando did not move during the shaking — it started about 30 seconds later. And in dilatant soils the drainage path is shorter still, because shear bands form and feed water in from the surrounding soil.

How accurately would you have to know the void ratio?

This is the question the whole method turns on. Residual strength goes as exp(−ψ/λe), so an error in void ratio is an error in ψ of the same size, and it comes out multiplied — hardest in exactly the tight, low-λ sands where sampling is most difficult.

Sampling disturbance, thawing strain in a frozen core, or simply the scatter between a sample and the ground beside it.

The point is not that testing is useless. Frozen sampling and careful laboratory work are still the best direct measurement available, and the book says so. The point is that a method whose answer moves by a factor of two for a void ratio error of one hundredth needs its uncertainty carried through to the design number — which is exactly what working in ψ, with a characteristic value chosen from a distribution, does instead.

12. How dense is dense enough?

This is the question Chapter 6 opened with, and it is the one a project actually has to answer: at what state does the conversation stop being about tolerable movements and start being about ground improvement? The book gives three thresholds from three directions, and they very nearly agree.

Why the design line is looser than the laboratory line. The laboratory threshold of ψ < −0.08 comes from isotropically consolidated samples, where every bit of the path to failure is undrained. Real ground is rarely at Ko=1; the usual range is 0.5–0.8, so a share of the loading path has already been taken drained and less excess pore pressure is available at the onset of instability. The book's conclusion is that ψ < −0.05 from laboratory testing should be conservative in practice — and that is the same line Module 2 shaded its logs with and Section 9 marks on its chart. The Ko arithmetic beside the slider is this page's own reading of that argument, not a published formula.
