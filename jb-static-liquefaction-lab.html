About this module

This is the chapter the whole book has been building toward: what actually happens when a contractive soil is sheared undrained, and how much strength survives on the other side. It opens with four real flow failures the book documents in detail — two static, two triggered by other events — because static liquefaction isn't a laboratory curiosity.

Fort Peck Dam

Montana, USA · 1938

Upstream slope of a hydraulic-fill dam failed near the end of construction, preceded by 12+ hours of visible bulging. The flowslide moved material up to 450 m, involving about 7.5 million m³ of fill, and killed 8 workers.

Nerlerk Berm

Beaufort Sea, Canada · 1983

An offshore sand berm for oil exploration liquefied under its own static loading during construction, repeatedly falling short of design elevation. Cost overruns exceeded $100 million. Extensively re-analyzed since — used as a real validation case throughout this book.

Aberfan

Wales, UK · 1966

A colliery spoil tip liquefied and flowed downhill, engulfing a village school. 144 people were killed, 116 of them children — from only about 107,000 m³ of material. One of the deadliest liquefaction failures on record.

Merriespruit

South Africa · 1994

A gold tailings dam was overtopped during a thunderstorm; erosion exposed loose saturated tailings inside the wall, which then liquefied. About 600,000 m³ of tailings flowed 3 km through the village below.

1. Peak strength, residual strength, and why brittleness is the real danger

The peak undrained strength of even very loose liquefiable sand isn't unusually low — typically su/p′o ≈ 0.15–0.30 (Fig. 6.10), comparable to normally consolidated clay. What makes static liquefaction dangerous is what happens after the peak: a large, brittle strength drop to the residual (critical-state) strength, which follows directly from the CSL definition:

sr/p′o = (M/2)·exp(−ψo/λe)   (Eq. 6.4)    IB = (su−sr)/su   (Bishop's brittleness index, Eq. 6.5)
Typical range 0.15–0.30 (Fig. 6.10) — not predicted by Eq. 6.4, set it directly.
Test G609 on Erksak 330/0.7 sand — the loosest clean-sand liquefaction test in the book's database (eo=0.800, p′o=500 kPa, ψo=+0.068) — is plotted as the reference point (default slider values). It showed an 80% peak-to-residual strength drop, with a smoothly mobilized stress ratio and pore pressure through the whole test — no sudden "collapse," which is the book's key evidence against the traditional collapse-surface picture (Section 2 below). Brittleness only develops once ψo/λe > 0.7 (the boundary of "normal consolidation" behavior, Fig. 6.14) — the badge below flags it.

2. Why "collapse surface" is the wrong picture

The traditional explanation for static liquefaction is a "collapse surface" or "instability line" — the idea that loose sand holds together in a metastable particle arrangement until a critical stress ratio triggers a sudden rearrangement. The book tested this directly against Test G609's data: the stress ratio and excess pore pressure both mobilize smoothly with strain, with no inflection at peak strength (ηL=0.62 at collapse) that would indicate a sudden structural event. A companion drained test on an even looser sample (e=0.820) showed no change in behavior at all when its stress path crossed that same "collapse" ratio.

NorSand simulations confirm why the collapse-surface idea is misleading: the stress ratio at instability isn't a fixed soil property at all. For the same critical friction ratio Mtc, varying only the ratio of plastic hardening to elastic rigidity (H/Ir) shifted the instability stress ratio ηL from 0.75 to 1.0 in simulations of real Guindon silty sand (33% fines, ψo=+0.065, Fig. 6.27). Liquefaction isn't a structural collapse — it's ordinary strain-softening plasticity that becomes catastrophic only when drainage can't keep up with pore pressure generation (Drucker's instability postulate, Eq. 6.20).

3. Predicting post-liquefaction residual strength — theory vs. current practice

The book's own recommended design tool (reproduced here exactly as the VBA function published in Fig. 6.48) blends the exact critical-state theory result (Eq. 6.4/6.17) for very loose soils with a conservative linear fit anchored to the case-history record for everything else:

sr/σ′vo = [(1+2Ko)/3]·(Mtc/2)·exp(−ψ/λe)   if ψ > λ10
sr/σ′vo = [(1+2Ko)/3]·0.0501·Mtc + 0.8·(λ10 − ψ)   otherwise

How dense is dense enough? Laboratory and case-history evidence converge on a practical threshold: soils denser than about ψ < −0.05 don't show the phase-transformation behavior that leads to damaging strength loss (Section 6.11) — Robertson's independent CPT-based criterion (Qtn,cs ≈ 70) lines up with the same boundary. Below this line the book frames the engineering conversation as "ground improvement"; above it, as "tolerable movements."
